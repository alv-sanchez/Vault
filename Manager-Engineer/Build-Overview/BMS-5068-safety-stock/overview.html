📦 New here? Inventory primer

The whole epic in everyday words

Gulf Distributing is a beverage distributor (DSD = Direct Store Delivery — they truck drinks straight to stores). Their warehouse holds cases of product. This epic is about not running out of a product, and not drowning in too much of it. Here are the ideas you need, in plain English.

Safety stock
Extra buffer inventory you keep on purpose, so you don't run out when a supplier ships late or a heat wave makes everyone buy Gatorade. It's the "just in case" cushion on top of normal stock.
DOH / DOI
Days on Hand / Days of Inventory — how many days your current stock will last at the current sales rate. "12 DOH" means "if we keep selling at today's pace, we have ~12 days before we hit zero." It's stock measured in time, not cases.
Velocity
How fast a product sells. Stored as Average_Daily_Depletion__c (cases sold per day). DOH is just stock ÷ velocity — the live value lives in Current_DOI__c.
Reorder point
The low-water mark. When stock drops to this line, the system raises its hand and says "refill me." Refilling is called replenishment.
Pick face
The front-of-shelf bin a warehouse worker (a "picker") grabs cases from to fill an order. It's small and gets refilled from bulk stock you already own.
Inbound PO
A Purchase Order — buying more product from the supplier (e.g. ordering more Red Bull from Red Bull). This is how new stock enters the building.

Option A (pick-face) is built and live. The open decision below is narrower now: should the supplier-level buffer (also built) also drive pick-face sizing, or does "supplier lead-time" really mean Option B (buy more), which is a from-scratch build elsewhere? Keep that one sentence in your pocket.

Go deeper — the targetCases formula, with a worked example

The formula turns a time target (days) into a physical order (cases). Read it left to right:

targetDOH = how many days of cover we want · Average_Daily_Depletion__c = units sold per day · Units_Per_Case__c = units in one case. Multiply the first two to get units we want on hand; divide by units-per-case to get cases.

Say:target = 10 days of cover velocity = 48 units/day (Average_Daily_Depletion__c) case size = 24 units/case (Units_Per_Case__c) units wanted10 × 48 = 480 units → cases480 ÷ 24 = 20 cases round to layerlayer = 8 cases → round 20 up to 24 (3 layers) clamp to binbin Max_Capacity__c = 22 → final = 22 cases

If targetDOH or velocity is null (an un-configured SKU), the formula is skipped entirely and the code falls back to today's behavior — fill the bin to its max. So turning this on can never make an un-configured product worse; it only changes products someone deliberately set a target for.

Go deeper — how S_InventoryThresholds.resolve() picks the target ("the engine that already shipped")

What "the engine already shipped (BMS-4609)" means: the hard part — deciding which DOH target applies to a given product at a given place — is already built and live on main. We are not rebuilding it. We only make its answer drive the order size.

The engine is three pieces working together:

  • SKU_Override__c — a hand-set, dated override for one exact product + location ("for this SKU in this warehouse, force target = 14 days until June 30").
  • Inventory_Threshold__c — the baseline targets, set broadly (per warehouse, and optionally per supplier via Account__c).
  • S_InventoryThresholds.resolve(itemId, locationId, accountId) — the referee. It returns Min/Target/Max DOH + Lead Time by walking a precedence ladder (most specific wins):

1. Active SKU_Override__c for this exact (item, location) — fills any field it sets.
2. Inventory_Threshold__c matching both supplier and warehouse.
3. Inventory_Threshold__c for the warehouse only.
4. Inventory_Threshold__c for the supplier only.
5. Nothing matches → returns null, and the caller decides the default.

Note step 2/4 need a supplier (accountId). Today every caller passes accountId = null (see E_ReplenishmentTask ~line 353), so the supplier tier exists but never fires — that's exactly why supplier-tier is "feasible but deferred."

🟠 Still open · the live product decision

Where does DOH order-sizing belong?

Same target DOH, two different builds in two different places. This is a product call, not a code call.

In plain English: a planner raises the safety-stock target for, say, Red Bull. That should mean "keep more on hand." But where? Option A says move more cases from bulk to the pick face inside the warehouse (using stock we already own). Option B says buy more from Red Bull on the next purchase order. Both are real, sensible readings of "more safety stock" — that's why this needs a human to decide, not code.

Go deeper — the pick-face-vs-PO fork in depth (why it's a genuine fork)

Picture the warehouse as two separate flows of inventory, each with its own "don't run out" risk:

Flow 1 — inside the building (Option A). You already own pallets of Red Bull in bulk storage. Through the day, pickers pull cases off the small pick face bin to build store orders. When that bin gets low, E_ReplenishmentTask makes a task: "move N cases from bulk to the pick face." Raising the DOH target here means refill the bin sooner / fuller so it never empties mid-shift. No new product enters the building — you're just shuffling what you have. This is cheap to build because the DOH value is already passed into this code today (it just nudges task priority, not quantity).

Flow 2 — from the supplier (Option B). Eventually bulk itself runs low and you must buy more from Red Bull. That's a purchase order. Here, safety stock is a reorder buffer: order enough extra to cover the supplier's lead time (how long shipping takes) plus demand swings, so you don't stock out waiting for the truck. This lives in purchasing (E_PurchaseOrder) and has no DOH wiring at all yet — it would be a from-scratch build.

Why this is a real product decision and not an engineering detail: the same words ("raise the buffer") point at two different systems, two different code paths, and two very different build costs. The epic's business language sounds like Flow 2 (suppliers, seasons, lead time). The cheap/ready path is Flow 1. Engineering can't pick — the business has to say which risk they're actually trying to kill, A, B, or both.

To unblock the build now, the engineer wires Option A (already-wired, low-risk) behind a feature flag and leaves an OPEN(refinement) marker in code at the exact wire-in point, so if the meeting chooses B, the seam is obvious.
